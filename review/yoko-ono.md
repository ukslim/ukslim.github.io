# Yoko Ono and the id: blank verse by AI

... in which I give someone else's ideas to AI, AI writes some good verse,
then I ramble on a bit around the subject.

First of all, let's get this out of the way:

{% include image url="twonks-ai-pizza.webp" w="500" caption="AI design logic (Twonks)" %}

I try very hard not to say "look what I made!".

I only say "look at this thing, which I think is good".

_I_ didn't make it.

(Apart from the block-quotes from ChatGPT, and quotes from Bluesky, this page is all my own work)

Anyway.

I fell into a harmless recreational habit of copy-pasting things into ChatGPT followed by "Rewrite this in iambic pentameter", for my own amusement. Leave me alone, it's less electricity than a cup of tea, and gives me more pleasure.

Or to put it another way:

> Sometimes I read a post on Bluesky’s feed,\
> And think: this might be better told in verse,\
> The kind that Shakespeare used, and Milton too,\
> In measured lines, unrhymed, of five strong beats.\
> I ask a bot to shape the thing anew,\
> To clothe its words in five iambic feet.\
> And often, to my pleasure and surprise,\
> The transformation makes me laugh enough\
> To share the lines, that others might enjoy.
{: .verse}

So I asked ChatGPT (High):

> Rewrite this in iambic pentameter.

> Before you do, if you need to, check with me for any unclear context. To
> preempt your first question, Sweep is the mute dog from Sooty and Sweep.

And ChatGPT churned away for a bit, then said:

> I think of Yoko as my patron saint\
> Of art that will not ask permission first.\
> Within us live two artists, each at war:\
> One studies crowds to learn what they might love;\
> The other, Yoko, sends a banshee's cry,\
> And does exactly what she wants to do.
>
> To hear her howling with the Beatles there,\
> I hear the screaming of the gathered fans.\
> Their cries were always part of what they played,\
> But none had thought to make them part of song.\
> Did Yoko know? I rather doubt she did.\
> She followed where her sudden impulse led,\
> And made a moment no design could make.
>
> To those who find her noise beyond the pale,\
> I offer Sweep, the dog of Sooty's world.\
> He has no words, but lets his squeaking fly,\
> And does precisely what his fancy bids.\
> Then put him in her place and hear anew:\
> At once the whole affair begins to sing.\
> Both voices spring from one ungoverned place.
>
> Then Mr Blobby barges through the door,\
> Another saint of this unruly creed.\
> What Freud would call the id has taken shape:\
> The oldest thing within us, deaf to rules;\
> No conscious thought can make its hunger wait.\
> It wants its wants fulfilled, and wants them now,\
> No tact, no plan, no care for what may break.\
> It wants, it cries, it takes, and then it laughs.
>
> And somewhere in that clamour, art gets made.
{: .verse}

(By the way, if you try this, do put GPT into high "thinking effort" -- it seems to loop through several times honing the metre, assessing tone, deciding where humour or gravitas is required, and so on, and the difference in quality is noticeable).

Now, I like this very much, but what am I to do with it? It's not my idea.
It's not my implementation. All I did was ask for it.

Had I taken Rob and Mark's conversation as inspiration for a poem, and written
the poem myself, I think that would conventionally be considered fair game. I would have no hesitation in saying "Hey Rob, I loved your thoughts about Yoko
Ono, look at the poem I've written!"

But because I didn't write it, AI did, I feel reluctant to show him. I'm not
100% sure I know Rob Manuel's opinions on AI creations, but I have a pretty good
idea of the general direction, and he might be very hostile indeed to this
thing I've done, by its very principle.

I am showing him, by the way. This page won't get shared with anyone else without
his permission.

What do I like about this verse? Well, what started out as a joke, has led me
into a newfound love of iambic pentameter. It has coincided with me audiobooking
the Emily Wilson translation of The Iliad, which is written in iambic pentameter. While the Greek original is in dactylic hexameter, Wilson says English cannot reasonably be written in that metre, and iambic pentameter gives the same sense of a strict metre, with a readable and speakable narrative pace.
I've found that almost everything I ask for in blank verse, comes out with a new
sense of gravitas. Read it aloud, and it just sounds more wise.

> Inside you are two artists, the one who thinks strategically about what the audience wants, and there's your inner Yoko that lets out a banshee howl and does exactly what they fancy in the moment.

... is an interesting observation.

> Within us live two artists, each at war:\
> One studies crowds to learn what they might love;\
> The other, Yoko, sends a banshee's cry,\
> And does exactly what she wants to do.
{: .verse}

... is an interesting observation that _sounds like Shakespeare_ or Milton or
translated-Homer, or whoever has that cultural stamp of approval. And it's
just, to me at least, very satisfying to read aloud.

I also greatly enjoy the irony of it being a treatise on the human id's
contribution to art, yes having been nowhere near a human id -- unless you
count my whim of asking for it.

I like one line in particular:

> It wants its wants fulfilled, and wants them now,
{: .verse}

A perfect fit to the metre, and that repetition of "want", which is absolutely
the point of the line.

Really, the core thing for me is, that's a verse I'd be very pleased with
had I written it. And being a very analytical person, I struggle to see why
I should like it less just because of how it came to be.

But, here's a thing. When I think about that line, I imagine how pleased a human
author would have been, to have come up with it. The author I'm imagining, of course, doesn't exist. I still have a hard time putting them out of my mind.

Almost certainly, the AI agent that produced the line didn't feel a spark of satisfaction. It's not quite true that ChatGPT "just predicts the next word" to do a job like this. Each time the LLM is invoked, that's really what it does. But lots of LLMs are invoked lots of times. Did one of those simulate the thoughts of a writer: "That bit's really good, keep that" or "what if I repeat 'want' three times, I like that idea". The tool doesn't let me see.

I think a lot of people are very attached to the idea of visualising the
act of creation, as they consume a work. While listening to a love song, they
imagine the lovelorn songwriter in their bedroom with a guitar and a biro. While reading a book (perhaps a certain kind of book?) they are experiencing it as a window into the author's personality. At some level, when you read Hemingway, you are thinking of Hemingway as a personality. Sometimes there's truth in that. Sometimes it's a fiction (think of authors with pen-names of the opposite gender to their own).

I think that for people with that attachment, the clear knowledge that no such human act of creation occurred is a real barrier to their enjoyment of the work.

I think that for some people, it's important that art is evidence of effort. "Wow, just think how long that must have taken to paint" is part of their pleasure in consuming art. Or "gosh they must have practiced for ages to get that good". These, I suppose, are the people who look at a pile of bricks in the Tate Modern and consider it unworthy.

Nobody practiced for ages to write that verse (although I suppose GPT's training took a long time and a lot of energy).

I've learned of one more category. Someone online (I can't find the exchange now)
told me that things created by humans have "something metaphysical about them".
I think they were talking about AI music. This person accepted that he'd be
unable to tell whether a lot of music was AI or human, in a blind test. But that
nonetheless AI music would be missing this metaphysical something.

For me, a person who doesn't believe in souls, that's supernatural woo.
But, if we openly tell people a work was created by AI (as we should), and that
causes them to think of it as lacking a soul -- that's a real effect.

---

I also want to look at this from the angle of people producing work. Art. Content. Whatever you want to call it. All the discourse about AI has brought
to my attention motivations I just hadn't thought of before.

I always thought, you make a cool thing, and you want other people to see the
cool thing and enjoy it. And I assumed that's why people made things - so that
the cool thing exists, and we can share our enjoyment of it.

But, evidently, per that pizza cartoon - for some people it's the praise that motivates them. The existence of the pizza isn't the prize: getting credit for its
creation is the prize.

I should admit: I like praise too. But I don't think I've earned praise for
that poem. (Some of my AI-generated fiction has more of my creative input, so
I wouldn't mind taking a _small_, _proportionate_ amount of praise).

The other one, that shouldn't surprise me, but sort of did, is money. I've seen
quite a lot of evidence that people are motivated by "make a thing with AI,
publish it, earn money". I can only imagine that this will peter out as people
realise there's no money there. You can still only make money by being significantly better than a rather high threshold.

In any case, that's academic for me since I have no intention of selling anything.

and to an extent I'm embarrassed to share it because there are creative people
I respect who would see it, and who are vehemently upset by AI. So it sits here seldom publicised.

What would Yoko do? What would Mr Blobby do?
