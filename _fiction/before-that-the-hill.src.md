*What follows is an edited transcript of a conversation between a writer (J) and an AI assistant (C, Claude Opus 5.5) on 17 and 18 November 2026, which produced the story "Before That, the Hill". It runs from J's first message to the point where J approved the draft. The workings were compiled afterwards, at J's request, by a later session of the assistant (Claude Sonnet 5.5) working from the conversation.*

*The conversation has been condensed. C's replies ran longer than they appear here and have been cut to what shaped the story. J's messages are given as typed. The draft itself is not reproduced, since it is the story. Web searching and reading of project files are summarised rather than reproduced. Conversation about the Gaelic names and about how the story handles OCD took place after the point where this transcript ends, and is not included.*

*The conversation sits inside a project begun with an earlier chat that produced the first story in the set, "Nice. Wrong.". J's sister gave him four Ardnamurchan miniatures and a card asking for one story per bottle, each featuring a pirate, a cook, a naturalist or a computer game designer. That earlier chat is not reproduced here. In it J had already said he wanted to save a "provenance trail" idea for a game designer in a later story.*

I really want to do the game designer obsessed with provenance, tying into the Blockchain provenance of the whisky. Not sure whether that can fit with Midgie or could be kept for the Sherry edition.

Maybe something where a midge is the hero, or the hero interacts with a midge, or midges. Or the game designer could make a game about a midge. Or the midge could be near the bottoms of a long chain of provenance in a game.

**C:** *[Reads the project's notes on the earlier story, then searches the web for the Midgie. It finds that the whisky is a blend of casks (the 2024 batch used bourbon, Sauternes, Madeira and sherry; batch two bourbon, port and sherry), that it was made with the Smidge repellent brand, and that it carries the same blockchain QR code as the rest of the range.]*

The Midgie suits the provenance idea well, because it is a blend of other casks. A story about a tree of origins could reach back to the other bottles, including the Madeira cask from the pirate story. Midges breed in wet, peaty ground, so a midge near the bottom of a chain of origins is physically plausible.

*[Offers four seeds for the game designer.]* A designer whose system tracks the history of every object, and who is broken by something that can't be given a history: a swarm. A designer who is bitten, and so becomes an entry in a ledger himself. A legendary item whose oldest record is a midge. And a small game in which you are one midge among a million.

*[Also offers the alternative: keep provenance for the Sherry bottle, with its solera system of ever-shrinking shares of old wine, and give the Midgie to the naturalist, who counts midges in traps for the midge forecast.]*

My lean is to keep the game designer on the Midgie and give the Sherry to the naturalist. Which of these appeals, or is it the swap?

## The chain

**J:** I think our Midgie story is a game designer building a super detailed world. And he's obsessive compulsive  so every object holds a record of it's source. This of course is ridiculous. So he's burning out from implementing all this pointless detail. And a midge is where he hits a wall. We need a long enough chain of items and their properties, ending in a midge.

Perhaps the midge has bitten multiple people, mingling their blood, breaking his inheritance system. But in a way a sane person would not care about?

Need to come up with a short story arc that is satisfying.

One option is a redemption where he chills out and makes a great game about a swarm of midges treated, sensibly, as identical dots. But I'm open to anything.

Cross-references to other whisky stories is optional and if present, light.

**C:** *[Proposes a chain: kipper, smoke, peat, larvae, one midge, then the midge's blood meals on a peat-cutter, a manse housekeeper and the player.]*

The real problem isn't mixed blood. It is that his system works like a family tree, where data only flows forward, and the midge makes a loop: the player has eaten a kipper that is partly made of the player. The silly part could be a single bug report from a tester: *"Guard arrested me for eating breakfast,"* because the midge took blood without asking, and a "stolen" flag passed down the chain to the fish.

*[Offers three endings.]* The swarm game, J's own idea, perhaps preceded by a research trip where he is eaten alive and finds not being able to tell one bite from another a relief; the risk is that it is too tidy. "The hill," where he adds one new value so that past a certain depth everything's source is simply the hill, a limit he chooses to accept; I think the most grown-up ending. Or the one player who looked: he ships without the midges, and months later a forum post shows someone found a deleted midge's ID still dangling in the data. Bittersweet, and it gives the obsession some value without approving of it. The last two could be combined.

On cross-references, the cook at the start of the chain is already a light nod to "Nice. Wrong.". One craft note: make the compulsion feel real and a bit painful, not just a joke, so that the ending lands.

Do you want this mostly comic with a soft landing, or melancholy with a comic surface?

## Names and setting

**J:** Ok, cool. Let's name the developer, and name the game under development. We seem to have already decided it's set in peaty Scotland, but expand on the type of game. Celtic RPG? It's crunch time - release date is soon.  So this bug report is a big deal. Some in the company want to drop the provenance system, believing it to be a waste of effort and not appealing to players.

Resolution is probably the Hill solution. But we can get some validation from players of the finished game following the trails.

**C:** *[Proposes names and a setting.]* The developer is Iain Gow, from the Gaelic *gobha*, a smith: a maker, in the middle of every chain. The game is *Dùthchas*, a Gaelic word for heritage or inherited nature, which gives a running joke that the studio calls it "Dutch" and marketing keeps proposing *Highland Fate*. It is an open-world RPG in an invented early-medieval Highlands with no magic, only weather, peat, cattle and feuds, announced with the line "every object remembers".

The provenance system is "the Telling": hand an object to a bard and they recite its line of descent, as old bards recited a chief's ancestry. That makes the midge loop sound absurd when read aloud. The studio is in Dundee, nine days from certification. The bug happens in the tutorial inn, in June, which is midge season, so every player is arrested at breakfast within ten minutes. The producer, Ruth, wants the Telling cut, and her case is fair: hardly anyone uses it twice, and the records make up a third of the save file.

*[Sets out an arc: the bug report; the meeting; the night in which every fix makes things worse; the hill; a coda on a forum of players who trace objects back to the hill and call themselves hillwalkers, with a small hidden detail of the bard pausing before "the hill".]*

Does Iain go up the real hill at the end, or stay at his desk?

## The hill

**J:** No need for climbing a hill.

So the "hillwalkers" are competing for the longest list before hitting the hill? That means the exit condition isn't length of chain but some other criterion. Need something plausible. But I love this.

**C:** You're right: if the hill came at a fixed depth, every chain would be the same length and there would be nothing to compete over. A better rule is that the Telling stops when it reaches a crowd. Iain stops keeping separate histories for anything that exists in bulk, such as midges, herring, grass, rain and peat larvae, and gives each kind one shared pool: the swarm, the shoal, the bog. When the bard reaches a pool, he says *"and before that, it came from the hill."*

It works three ways. The loop goes away, because blood goes into "the swarm" and not a particular midge, so the player is no longer their own ancestor. The saves shrink, so Ruth gets her numbers. And it means something: individuals have a line of descent and crowds are the hill, which is the moment Iain lets go, written into his own code.

Chains then differ in length because a kipper is short, while an object made and handled only by named people can run for dozens of steps. So the hillwalkers' competition is for objects with a long human history, and players become historians of the NPCs' lives. Some start gaming it, which the community argues over. That is also how the story vindicates Iain: players don't care about the herring, they love the people.

*[Adds an optional joke for the coda: finding the longest chain that never doubles back is a famously intractable problem, so no record can be proven the longest.]*

## The draft

**C:** *[Reads the project's Kladde 26 materials and the published text of "Nice. Wrong." to match its conventions, then writes the draft and sends it as a markdown file of about 2,400 words.]*

The draft is a magazine-style feature, reported after the game's release. A few choices to push back on:

- The story opens with the bug ticket laid out as a table and ends with the same ticket closed, which echoes the batch cards in "Nice. Wrong." without copying them.
- The loop isn't the player's. For the player's blood to be in that morning's kipper, a midge would have to hatch, the peat be cut and the fish smoked overnight. So the loop belongs to Seumas, the peat-cutter, built up over the game's 200 years of simulated history, and the player only meets the stolen flag.
- Tomasz, a junior engineer, suggests the pooling and Iain refuses on principle. Peigi, the Gaelic consultant, changes his mind with how the old genealogists stopped, and her grandmother's saying. It is Iain's relief, but someone else gives it to him.
- Your idea of the midges as identical dots comes back as part of his release.
- The cross-reference is one light echo of a line from "Nice. Wrong." about a biscuit telling you where it had been.
- Two details to check before publishing: the MacDonald descent through Somerled to Conn of the Hundred Battles is a real traditional claim, and the grandmother's "came from the hill" is invented as a family saying.
