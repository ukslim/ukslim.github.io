For my birthday, my sister gave me a box of four Ardmurchan whisky miniatures, and a card saying: _"Write one short story inspired by each whisky. Each one must feature either a pirate, a cook, a naturalist or a computer game designer. Happy birthday!"_

This story features a cook and is inspired by Ardnamurchan AD/, which was nice and not wrong.

Monday, so the restaurant is shut and I have the kitchen to myself. I have decided to make a biscuit I last ate about thirty years ago, and to keep a card for every attempt — I know what I'm like and I will otherwise make the same mistake four times.

The first problem is that I don't know what it was called. The biscuits came out of the barrel on the worktop, a brown stoneware thing with a cork-lined lid, and I never once saw a packet. What I have is this. It was rectangular and about as long as my finger was then. It was pale brown, with rows of small holes and a raised border, and as far as I can remember nothing written on it at all. It tasted of malt. It was not very sweet. It broke cleanly but gave a little in the middle.

I ate two of them most afternoons after school for about four years. That is something like fifteen hundred biscuits, which is a better sample than I get for most things I'm asked to reproduce. I taste a sauce in somebody else's restaurant and I can usually write down what is in it before the plate is cleared. This ought to be a fortnight's work.

Today's was a guess to give me somewhere to start: a shortbread, more or less, with a spoonful of malt extract. It came out the colour of good toast. It is short and buttery and I would be happy to put it on a plate next to a coffee and charge for it. I ate four standing at the pass.

It is nothing like. It's far too rich. The biscuit I'm after is plainer than this and tastes of grain before it tastes of butter. So "Nice" is only fair, and "Wrong" is the part I need to pay attention to.

The last three went wrong in the same direction, so this month has been about the malt.

The extract from the supermarket is mostly sweetness. It gives you the smell of malt as you open the oven and then very little in the mouth. On Thursday I went out to a home-brew shop in a unit behind a tyre fitter's, where a man in a fleece spent twenty minutes explaining the difference between the malts on his shelves and seemed disappointed that I only wanted to bake with them. He sold me a tub of a darker extract and a kilo of crushed malted barley. I toasted the barley in a dry pan until it smelled like the crust of a loaf and put it through the spice grinder.

It helps to write down what I'm actually chasing. It is toasty, not sweet. It's something like a digestive with the wheatiness taken out. There is a warmth right at the back, the way the last corner of a piece of toast tastes, and under that a very faint saltiness. It's a dry sort of flavour, and it stays for a while after you have swallowed.

Batch 4 has the front of that. You bite it and for a second it's right, and then the butter takes over and the malt goes missing. The texture is still wrong as well. Mine snap. I've written in the margin that the real ones may never have been properly crisp. The lid of that barrel didn't sit flat, the cork had shrunk, and it lived a foot from the kettle. Anything kept in it for a week would have taken on some damp. I may have been trying to bake a fresh version of something I only ever met stale.

It was a factory biscuit. I've known this for about a fortnight and have been cooking around it.

The holes were too regular for anything else, and nobody in our house baked. There would not have been butter in it. We were a margarine household, the hard kind in a block, and whoever made those biscuits by the million was not using anything better. So I have spent two months improving on something that was never very good, and the job now is to bake worse, on purpose and accurately. That is harder than it sounds. I had to stand over the granulated sugar for a while before I could bring myself to weigh it.

The margarine I remember was Kingcup, in gold foil with a yellow flower on it. My wholesaler laughed at me. I found a mention of it on a forum for people who collect old packaging, where somebody said a newsagent in Harrow Weald still stocked it, so last Monday I spent an hour and ten minutes getting there, the last part of it on a bus. It was a corner shop on a parade, with a chest freezer by the door. There were six blocks in the chiller, between the lard and the suet. The man told me he orders it in for three customers, and that I could be the fourth if I was going to be regular about it.

On the shelf behind him there was a row of Ransome's vanilla flavouring, the small bottle with the red cap. I recognised it before I knew I remembered it. It lived in the cupboard above our cooker. I bought two.

One other change. I've been tasting these at eleven in the morning, when I happen to have them out of the oven, and I always ate the originals at about ten past four, before anyone had put the kitchen light on. A palate is not the same in the afternoon. From now on I'm tasting at ten past four and writing it on the card.

My sous chef tried one, said it was fine, and asked whether I was all right.

I have a name. It was an Aldwick Malt, made by Garside & Lowe.

There is a woman in Stockport who sells old biscuit wrappers, flattened, in plastic sleeves. I went through four hundred photographs on her page on my phone during a quiet Tuesday lunch, and I knew the border when I saw it. I bought a wrapper from 1991 for six pounds. It has the ingredients on the back in descending order, which to anybody in my trade is most of a recipe: wheat flour, vegetable fat, sugar, barley malt extract, partially inverted sugar syrup, salt, raising agent, flavouring.

On the end flap there is a code, L1 204 B, and on a whim I rang the careline of the company that owns the Garside & Lowe name now. I expected a script. I was passed to a woman called Maureen, who has been there thirty-one years and read it to me straight off the flap. It means Line 1 at the Wigan factory, the two hundred and fourth day of the year, B shift, which was afternoons. She could tell me who the supervisor on that line would have been. She told me the flour always came from the same mill, eight miles up the road, and that the factory closed in 2004.

I had not understood until then that a biscuit could tell you where it had been. You only have to find the person who can read it.

The mill is still there. They sold me a sack of their biscuit flour, which is softer than anything I keep, and sixteen kilos is more than I will ever need. I also bought the barrel, or its twin, from an auction site. Each batch now sits in it for two days before I go near it.

One more adjustment. As a child I had nothing I was supposed to be doing between getting in and about six o'clock, so I was not eating them in a hurry, and I have been tasting these between a delivery and a prep list. From now on I sit down.

It is very close. There is one thing left, and I'm fairly sure it's the fat. Maureen says they never used a margarine at all.

I have matched the water, which is not a sentence I expected to write when I started this.

The shortening came in a box big enough to sit on. The malt extract is from the maltster the mill has always sent its customers to. The stamp took the engraver three weeks, working from the wrapper and from a drawing I made of the holes, and when I pressed the first one and lifted the brass away I had to go and stand in the yard for a minute.

It came out of the barrel this afternoon looking exactly as it should. It was the right colour, and it broke the right way, cleanly, with a slight give in the middle. I sat down with it at ten past four with the lights off. The extraction was running, so I got up and switched that off as well. At home I would have had one ear on the gate, and you can't listen for anything over a fan.

As far as I can establish, it is the same biscuit.

It isn't right, and I can't say how. Nobody else can either. Nobody else ate them out of that barrel at that hour. I am the only instrument there is for this, and there is nothing to check me against.

I have been through the card twice. There is nothing left on it to change.

I've left the last row empty. I don't know which word goes in it.

I haven't baked a biscuit since September. The barrel is on the shelf above the aprons and has elastic bands in it.

This afternoon there was a block and a half of butter in the walk-in with Monday's date on it and half a jar of malt extract that nobody else is ever going to use, so I made biscuits for staff tea. I didn't get the cards out. I didn't need them, because it was the first one, and I didn't weigh the malt. They went in at three and I forgot about them until the timer went.

Then we did seventy covers across two sittings. The dishwasher stopped at eight. A table of twelve arrived as fourteen. Somebody sent back a piece of turbot, and they were right to.

Afterwards I went out to the back step to wait for Dev, who brings his car round on Saturdays and drops me home. It was properly cold for the first time this year, cold enough to see my breath. I had two of the biscuits in my apron pocket, wrapped in blue roll.

I ate one. It was short, and the butter came first. The malt arrived late and stayed, toasty and a little dry, with a bit of salt behind it. One edge had caught in the oven, and that was the best part.

It was nothing like an Aldwick Malt. I ate the second one.

The kitchen was clean behind me and somebody else was locking up. Nobody wanted anything. I sat there with one ear on the road until I heard the car.

The rest are in a tub by the pass, where I expect they'll last until about Tuesday. I filled in the card when I got home. It didn't take long.
