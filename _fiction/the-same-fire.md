# The Same Fire

## I. The Journey

### 1994

The number came through at half six. Darren's mate's mate had left it on a voicemail — a mobile number, which you rang and got another number, which you rang and got a bloke who said junction 17 then the B-road toward Lambourn, look for the vans.

We were in Darren's Fiesta by seven. Me in the front, Nic in the back with her knees up because something had gone wrong with the passenger seat and it only reclined now, it didn't sit up. The gearbox whined in third. We had two litres of Volvic, a packet of Silk Cut, three wraps of speed and two pills that Darren said were Mitsubishis but looked like paracetamol that someone had breathed on.

The M4 was empty going west. Friday evening, wrong direction. Everyone else heading into London for the weekend and us heading out of it, into the dark, into the country. Nic had her feet on the window. She said she'd heard Carl Cox was playing but she said that every time. The tape deck didn't work so she was singing Strings of Life out of tune with her eyes closed.

I didn't know what I was driving toward. That was the point.

### 1394

We left after the second milking and walked west.

Huw and me, and Huw's sister Efa who was faster than both of us on the hill. The light was still good. It would be good for hours yet — the eve of Calan Mai, the longest twilight, the year tipping over into its bright half. Huw had a skin of mead from his father's store, taken without asking. I had bread and hard cheese in my shirt. Efa had nothing except the half-smile she wore when she knew something you didn't.

The way was over the ridge and down through the beech wood and then up again to the high meadow where the fires would be lit. We'd heard it talked about since the lambing. A gathering. Not a village thing — bigger, wilder, people from three or four parishes coming together where the old track crossed the ridge. Huw's cousin had been the year before and said the drums went on till morning.

The ground was soft from rain. The woods smelled of wild garlic and rot. In the clearings the bluebells had come through in carpets so thick they looked like water. We walked fast and didn't talk much. Efa found mushrooms at the wood's edge — the small brown ones that grew in the dung, the ones the priest called the devil's bread. She picked them without comment and wrapped them in a dock leaf.

We were ten miles from home. The ridge ahead of us was black against a sky that wouldn't go dark.

## II. Arrival

### 1994

You heard it before you saw it. That was always how it worked. The road narrowed, hedgerows close on both sides, and then there was a bass frequency that wasn't so much a sound as a pressure change. A pulse in the car's bodywork. Nic sat up. Darren said *here we go* and turned off the headlights because there were already cars pulled up on the verge, and torches, and people walking in the same direction along the lane like some kind of pilgrimage.

The field was enormous. Two rigs, maybe three, set up at the far end against a line of trees, and between us and them a broken landscape of cars and vans and people moving in the half-dark. No structure to it. No entrance, no perimeter. You just walked in off the lane and you were there.

The sound was coming from the big rig — a stack of speakers taller than a man, black cabinets, trembling. The DJ was up on a platform behind them, headphones on, head down, just a silhouette against a bank of lights that swept the crowd in programmed arcs. The bass was so low you felt it in your bowels before your ears made sense of it. A four-to-the-floor kick that reorganised your heartbeat.

There were maybe five hundred people and it was early yet. They were scattered across the field in clusters, some dancing already, some standing about with cans. No one was in charge. That was the whole of it.

### 1394

We smelled the smoke first, then saw the glow.

Over the last rise the high meadow opened up and the fires were already lit — two great bonfires set thirty paces apart, and between them a corridor of heat and light that rippled the air. People were arriving from every direction, up every track and path. More people than I had ever seen in one place. I tried to count but gave up past a hundred. Families and young men in groups and women with their hair loose and children underfoot.

The cattle had been driven through already. You could see the hoof-churned mud between the fires and smell the singed hair. The beasts were away on the far slope, lowing and unsettled. That was the purpose. That was what the elders would tell you — the purification of the herd, the blessing of the new season.

But the drums had started.

Two men with frame drums the size of a cartwheel, seated near the larger fire, and they were not playing for the cattle. The sound was steady and faster than a heartbeat — the pace you'd walk at if you were angry, if you were looking for a fight. A woman near them was singing, or chanting, the same phrase over and over in a melody that went nowhere, that circled back on itself like a dog finding its place to lie down. It wasn't beautiful. It was insistent.

No one was in charge. The priest from Huw's parish was not there. The lord's reeve was not there. The people who kept order in the daylight had stayed in the daylight. Here it was firelight and noise and the smell of roasting meat and something under it — sweat and smoke and crushed grass and the sweet sourness of mead already spilled.

Efa divided the mushrooms into three and we ate them with the last of the bread to kill the taste. I drank mead and watched the fires.

## III. Warming Up

### 1994

The first pill took forty minutes. That was normal. You stood around feeling stupid, smoking, talking rubbish to Darren about some girl he'd met in the kebab shop, checking your jaw for tension like a hypochondriac. Nic was already dancing, because Nic didn't need chemicals, she just needed a beat. She danced with her eyes closed and her arms up and a look on her face like she was trying to remember something. She was better at this than us. She'd done the Haçienda when she was sixteen with a fake ID and she treated raving with the seriousness of someone who'd found their religion early.

Then it hit. Not like a wave, not like people say. More like a lock turning. One second the music was external — just noise, pleasant noise, but outside you. Then the lock turned and it was inside you and the bass was your pulse and the hi-hat was your breathing and the distance between you and the five hundred other people in the field closed to nothing.

I started moving. Not dancing yet. Moving. Shifting weight, loosening. The speed was underneath it, giving everything a bright clean edge, and the pill was on top, opening some valve in the chest that was usually closed. I wanted to talk to everyone. I wanted to tell Darren I loved him, which I would never say sober because we'd been mates since we were twelve and you didn't say that. I didn't say it. But I could have.

The DJ — not Carl Cox, obviously, some bloke in a hoodie — dropped a track that started with a single acid line, raw and spiralling, and the crowd noise went up and Nic grabbed my arm and pulled me in toward the stack and I stopped thinking about anything.

### 1394

The mushrooms crept in slowly, the way dawn comes.

At first the fire seemed brighter but that might have been the darkness deepening around it. Then the edges of things began to breathe — the flames not just flickering but swelling and contracting like a living chest. Huw was laughing at nothing. I was not laughing. I was listening.

The two drummers had been joined by a third, and a boy with a pipe — three-holed, high-pitched, cutting through the drum-sound the way a bird cuts through wind. The rhythm had settled into something that my body recognised before my mind did. My feet were moving. I hadn't told them to. The ground was uneven and wet and my feet were finding the beat in it, pushing into the mud on the downstroke, lifting on the off-beat, and the fire was doing the same thing, breathing with it, and the dark was close and full of people doing the same thing.

There was no dance to it. No figure. Nobody was leading. The people nearest the fire were moving and the movement spread outward like ripples in a pool, and where two ripples met people adjusted without looking at each other, without speaking, just bodies in the same pulse finding a way not to collide. Efa was somewhere in it already — I caught a glimpse of her, face lit orange, hair wild, feet bare. She'd taken her shoes off and her feet were black with mud.

The mead was going round in skins and cups and I drank and the warmth of it met the strangeness of the mushrooms and something shifted. The chanting woman had been joined by others and the sound they made was not a song. It was a drone — a single note held by eight or ten voices, wavering, with the drum-beat underneath it like a floor you could stand on. I felt it in my breastbone.

I stopped thinking about anything.

## IV. The Peak

### 1994

There's a point — and if you know, you know, and if you don't, I can't explain it — where the music stops being something you hear and becomes something that happens to you. The DJ had been building for twenty minutes, layering, stripping back, layering again, and the crowd had gone with him, pulled forward and held back, pulled forward and held back, until the tension was unbearable, until five hundred people were leaning into the same future beat, willing it, and then he dropped it.

And the field came apart.

I don't mean violence. I mean the opposite of violence. I mean five hundred people screaming with joy and jumping and the ground shaking and the bass so heavy it distorted the air and for a few seconds — I swear this is true — I didn't know where I ended and the crowd began. I was just a node in it. A cell in a body.

The lights were sweeping in great arcs and every time they caught a face it was the same face — eyes closed, mouth open, transported. Fat lads with their shirts off. Skinny girls in dungarees. A bloke who must have been fifty, dancing like his skeleton had come loose. Nic, three feet away from me, tears running down her face, grinning. Darren behind her, hands in the air, shouting something I couldn't hear over the music, which had become the only thing that existed.

Time broke. I don't mean it slowed down or sped up. I mean it stopped being a line and became a space. I was in the music the way you're in weather — surrounded, immersed, without reference points. A track ended and another began and I couldn't tell you when. I was not thinking. I was not in my head at all. I was in my legs, my chest, my jaw, the bass, the crowd, the dark.

It could have been two hours or twenty minutes. Both are true.

### 1394

The drums got faster.

That is the simple truth of it, and everything else followed from that. The three drummers found each other's rhythm and then pushed it, notch by notch, and the crowd — I will call it a crowd, though no one had summoned it, no one had commanded it, it had assembled itself — the crowd followed. The feet moved faster. The drone-voices rose. The pipe climbed in pitch until it was barely music, until it was a bright hot wire of sound stretched across the top of the drum-pulse.

The fires had burned down to their cores and the light was red and low and the faces in it were not faces I knew. I mean that. I looked at Huw and he was Huw but he was also something older, something underneath Huw, and the expression on his face was not any expression I had seen him make in all our years of knowing each other. His mouth was open. His eyes were closed. He was hitting the ground with his feet as if he hated it, as if he was trying to break through it to whatever was underneath, and the drums were with him, and the drums were winning.

A woman near the fire was spinning. Not dancing — spinning, arms out, head back, round and round, and she didn't fall. The firelight caught her and lost her and caught her and lost her and she became a kind of time-keeping, a human wheel, and I could not look away from her and I could not tell you how long she spun for.

The mushrooms had taken me somewhere I did not have words for. The fire was not just light. It was alive and it was speaking in a language I almost understood, a language made of heat and rhythm, and my body understood it even though my mind was a dog chasing its tail around the edges. I was not afraid. I was — I was not anything. I was the drumbeat. I was the mud under my feet. I was the smoke going up. I was not a person with a name.

I was trying to break through to whatever was underneath and the drums were with me and the drums were winning.

Efa found me at some point — or I found her — and she was in the same place, the same not-place, and she grabbed my hands and we moved together, not dancing, just moving, and her grip was the only thing that told me I still had a body. Her fingers were cold and strong and the rest of me was heat and rhythm and nothing else.

## V. Stepping Out

### 1994

I came up for air around three. You had to. Your body made you — legs gone, mouth dry, a ringing in your ears that sat on top of the music like a second frequency. I walked away from the rig toward the tree line and the music receded behind me and the world came back in pieces.

The night air was cold on my skin and I hadn't realised how hot I'd been. I was drenched. My T-shirt was a rag. I stood at the edge of the field and drank the last of the water and smoked a cigarette and my hands were shaking.

The stars were out. That's the thing about these fields in the middle of nowhere — you look up and there's no light pollution and the sky is insane, thick with stars, and you stand there with a forty-thousand-watt sound system two hundred yards behind you pumping acid techno into a field in Berkshire and above you the Milky Way is doing what it's been doing for thirteen billion years and the scale of it makes you want to laugh or cry and because of the chemicals you do both.

Darren materialised next to me. He'd been sick. He looked terrible and happy. He said *that second pill was alright actually* and I said *yeah* and we stood there for a bit, not talking, just breathing. From this distance the rig looked like a small bright wound in the dark and the sound was still heavy but bearable and the crowd was a single moving thing, a organism, a murmuration.

Nic came over and sat on the grass and said her feet were destroyed. She took her trainers off and her socks were soaked through. She lay back and looked at the stars and said *I could do this forever* and none of us said anything because we knew she meant it and we knew she couldn't.

### 1394

I walked away from the fire to piss and the silence hit me like a wall.

Not silence. The drums were still going. But the distance made them small and the night was vast and I was standing at the edge of the meadow in the wet grass with the stars above me in such profusion that the sky looked broken, looked wounded, as if someone had taken a white-hot nail to a sheet of black leather and punched a thousand holes.

My legs were shaking. I was drenched in sweat and the night air bit into it and I stood there steaming like a horse. My feet hurt. I hadn't noticed until now. The mushrooms were ebbing and the world was becoming solid again, re-acquiring its edges, and the fire from this distance was a small bright wound in the dark and the people around it were a single moving thing.

Huw found me. He was breathing hard and his face was mud-streaked and he looked like something dug up. He said nothing. He sat in the grass and we shared the last of the mead and it was warm and sweet and the taste of it was the most ordinary thing that had happened in hours and I was grateful for it.

Efa came and lay in the grass and said her feet were ruined. She held them up and they were black and cut and she didn't care. She looked at the sky and said something in Welsh that I didn't catch, something about the fire still burning overhead, and then she was quiet.

The drums had not stopped. From here they sounded like a heartbeat heard through a wall — someone else's heartbeat, or the hill's, or the night's. I could still feel it in my chest.

## VI. Going Back In

### 1994

You always went back in. That was the unwritten law. You rested and you drank water and you smoked and then you went back in because the music hadn't stopped and your body still wanted it and there were hours left before dawn.

The second wave was different from the first. Looser, slower, less frantic. The DJ had shifted into something deeper — less hands-in-the-air, more head-down, eyes-closed. Detroit stuff. Long builds over rolling bass lines that didn't peak, that just kept going, that were the musical equivalent of a road at night. I danced differently now. Less jumping, more swaying. My body had found a lower gear and it wanted to stay there.

The crowd had thinned but the ones who were left were the real ones, the ones who'd come for this specific thing, and there was a quality to it now that was almost peaceful, almost sacred, though I'd never have used that word. A communion. Three in the morning in a field and two hundred people moving together in the dark and the music holding all of them like a hand and nobody speaking, nobody needing to speak, because the music was saying everything and we were all hearing the same thing.

Nic was next to me and she was crying again, silently, still dancing, and I put my arm around her shoulders and she leaned into me for a second and then straightened up and kept going and that was enough, that was the whole conversation.

### 1394

We went back.

The crowd had thinned but the fire was still burning and the drums had not stopped. I think new drummers had taken over — the rhythm was the same but the hands were different, steadier, less urgent. The chanting had thinned to three or four voices and the pipe had gone but the drone remained, held by women who seemed to breathe in turns so that the note never broke, never wavered.

I danced differently now. The wildness had burned out of me and what was left was something slower and more certain. My feet knew the ground. The mud had a pattern to it, compressed and recompressed by a hundred feet, and my body knew where to step without looking. I was tired in a way that went past tiredness into something clean on the other side of it.

Huw was near me, moving slowly, his eyes open now but looking at nothing. Efa was with the women who were holding the drone and her voice was in it, part of the long unbroken note, and I could not pick her voice out from the others but I knew it was there.

The fire was low and red and the heat of it was gentle now, a warmth rather than a force, and the people who were left were the ones who had come for this, who had walked ten miles and eaten the devil's bread and danced in the mud until their feet bled, and we were all hearing the same thing.

There is a word the old people use. *Hiraeth*. It means a longing for something you cannot name. I felt it then and I did not know what I longed for because I was in the middle of having it.

## VII. Dawn

### 1994

The sky went grey in the east and then pink and then the sun came up over the tree line and hit the field and we saw what we looked like.

Mud. Sweat. Cigarette burns in T-shirts. Pupils the size of pennies. Faces that had been through something. The field was trashed — cans, bottles, fag ends, a shoe, someone's jacket, the scattered remnants of five hundred people's night. The rigs were still going but the music had become ambient, vague, a comedown soundtrack, and people were drifting toward their cars in ones and twos with the careful gait of the chemically depleted.

Darren was asleep in the car. He'd gone back at some point and reclined the driver's seat and passed out with his mouth open and he looked about twelve. Nic and I sat on the bonnet and watched the sunrise and didn't talk. The comedown was starting — a greyness at the edges, a cooling, a sense of something receding that you couldn't hold onto. The birds were singing. They'd probably been singing for hours but the music had hidden them and now they were the loudest thing and they didn't care about us, they were doing what they'd been doing since before there were people to hear them.

Nic said *same time next month?* and I said *obviously* and she put her head on my shoulder and I could feel her heartbeat through her arm, still fast, still going, not quite ready to be ordinary again.

We drove home in silence. The M4 was filling up with Saturday-morning traffic and we slotted into it like a message in a bottle returning to the sea. Three kids in a shit car coming back from somewhere that didn't exist anymore. The field was just a field again. We were just people again. The lock had turned back.

But I had been on the other side of it and I knew the door was there.

### 1394

The sky lightened in the east and the fire died to embers and the drums stopped.

That was the strangest moment. After hours of rhythm the silence was a physical thing, a pressure, an absence with weight. My ears rang. The birds were singing — they had been singing for some time, maybe, but the drums had hidden them and now they were the only sound and they did not care about us.

The meadow was churned to black mud. The two bonfires were ash and ember, still breathing heat. The people who remained were grey-faced and hollowed out and sitting or lying in the wet grass with the careful stillness of those who have been through something they cannot yet speak about. An old woman was collecting the drums, wrapping them in cloth. Children who had slept through the worst of it were waking and crying.

Huw was asleep in the grass with his arms crossed over his chest like a dead man in a story and his face peaceful and filthy. Efa and I sat by the embers and watched the sky brighten and did not talk. Her voice was gone from the chanting. She opened her mouth and nothing came out and she smiled about it, a cracked and ruined smile, and shrugged.

We would walk home in the light. Ten miles over the ridge with the sun on our backs and the ordinary world reassembling itself around us — the sheep, the stone walls, the track down to the village, the church, the priest, the hundred small constraints of a life lived in the sight of others. My feet would bleed into my shoes. Huw would sleep for a day. Efa would not speak of it. By evening we would be who we had been before.

But I had been on the other side of it. The fire was the same fire it had always been. The rhythm was the same rhythm. The need to be in it — to stop being a single creature and become part of the pulse of something older and larger — that need was not something the night had created. The night had only answered it.

The sun came up. We walked home. The drums were still in my chest.
